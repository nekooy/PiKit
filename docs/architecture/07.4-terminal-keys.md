# The terminal's key bar

*[Architecture](../ARCHITECTURE.md) §7.4.*

## The terminal's key bar: a scrolling row of keys and three actions

The bar is **one row of keys and three round buttons**, drawn on the terminal's own dark rather than
on the app's light palette — the page behind it is black, and a light strip along its bottom edge read
as a second surface rather than as part of the terminal.

The keys are `滚动` (the follow-the-output toggle), `↑`, `↓`, `←`, `→`, `ESC`. `滚动` leads because it
is a *state* and belongs where the thumb already is; the arrows are what a soft keyboard does badly;
`ESC` ends the row because a shell asks for it least. The three round buttons are **not** keys — they
act on the bar and on the input path rather than sending a byte: send-text, the app's own keyboard, and
the IME.

**The dark reaches the window's last pixel.** The bar's own background was never the last
surface on screen: the page's scaffold left its `surface` — #F7F7F7 in the light scheme — in the
navigation inset below the bar, so the bar sat on a white strip 63 px tall on the 1080 px
emulator, with the gesture handle drawn on it. For a while the page painted that strip through a
`PiPage` parameter of its own; the parameter is gone, because no page ends above the strip any
more (§9.2's page frame). The bar owns it now: its black is drawn first and the strip's height is
padding *inside* that background, so the shell reaches the window's last pixel while the keys stay
above the handle. The page still raises `DarkNavigationBar()` so the handle is light while it is
composed; the general rule is §13's *The window's last pixels are a page's, not the system's*. The
top bar is deliberately not included: the header names the shell and belongs to the app's chrome,
and the page's blackness is its body.

### The keys scroll, and each is as wide as its label

An earlier version divided the row into nine equal slots so every key always fit. It fitted, and it
was wrong: on a 360dp screen nine slots are 25dp each, which is a key a thumb misses, and the bar
exists to save typing — a key that cannot be hit is worse than one that is off screen. The row is a
`horizontalScroll` now, each key is sized by its label, and the three actions stay outside it because
an action that slid off the edge is a control the reader cannot find. A key is a `Button` whose width
is its own label's past a 38dp floor, and whose ink is 32dp tall — the platform's own 48dp minimum
touch target pads that out to the row's real height, measured at 126 px on the 420dpi emulator. Every
label is drawn in one style, so two keys cannot disagree about how tall a key is, and nothing has to
be measured to arrange them.

### The three round buttons

**Send text** opens a field and sends what is typed there to the terminal **exactly as typed**. It
used to append a carriage return, so the string was *run*; that was wrong for the case that matters —
a `git commit -m` message is written to be reviewed or completed, not executed the instant it is
pasted. Measured: `echo noenter` typed in the sheet lands on the prompt's line with the cursor after
it and no output line.

**The app's own keyboard** (the laptop button) raises a panel of keys above the bar: five rows from `Esc`
and the digits to `Alt`, the arrows and `Space`. It is not an IME — nothing inserts text into a field;
every tap is a byte sequence handed to the pty, so the terminal's own line editing, history and
`Ctrl+C` are what the reader is talking to. The three modifiers are **latches**: they stay down for one
key and then let go, which is what a desk keyboard does with a chord and what a phone cannot do with
two thumbs. `Ctrl` maps a letter to its control code, `Alt` prefixes the escape byte, `Shift`
uppercases or picks a digit's shifted symbol, and all three can be latched at once. Measured on the
device: `Ctrl` then `c` prints `^C` and a fresh prompt, `echo` + `Space` + `hi` + `↵` runs.

The panel is a rounded card and it is the bar's **sibling in the page's column**, not an
overlay: the terminal view is measured above it, so the panel pushes the shell's last lines up
instead of covering them.

The bar's inner row has **no leading padding**, and the key row has no clip of its own. The pill's
own rounded clip is what hides a half-scrolled key on the leading side — which is where it
belongs, because the arc is already there. An earlier version put the keys five dp inside the
pill and rounded *them*: the left cut then fell inside the arc as a straight vertical line,
which reads as a rendering fault rather than as "there is more to the left".

The **trailing** side is the other way round, and its inset is arithmetic rather than taste. The
actions are 34dp circles in a 44dp pill, so the end cap is a half-circle of 22dp; with no inset a
circle of 17dp pokes 5dp past it and the clip flattens its right side — measured as the circle's
colour running to x=1057 against a pill that ended at 1058, which is what "the keyboard button is
flush against the edge" was. At exactly 5dp the circle is **concentric** with the cap: the gap is
then the 5dp the keys have above and below, and no pixel is clipped. Smaller flats the edge,
larger cuts it.

The gap the first key needs at rest is a **spacer inside the scrolling content**, not padding on
the viewport: an inset would scroll the cut with the keys and put the straight edge back inside
the pill, while a spacer scrolls away and leaves the arc to do the hiding.

On the trailing side a cut key is **erased** rather than painted over, and the shape of the erase
is the one the implementations that exist converge on: a **mask**, not an overlay, over a *short
fixed* length. Android's own fading edge is this at a fixed `fadingEdgeLength`
(`View.setHorizontalFadingEdgeEnabled`), and `GIGAMOLE/ComposeFadingEdges` defaults to a 16dp
`FadeClip` whose stops — 0, 0.5 and 1 with half alpha at the midpoint — are a linear ramp. Compose
Foundation 1.12.1 has no modifier for it (nothing in the artifact matches `fad`), so the recipes
are hand-rolled `drawWithContent` masks; this one is `CompositingStrategy.Offscreen` plus a
`BlendMode.DstOut` rect. Over the bar there is nothing to see and over a key there is nothing left.

Painting the pill's own colour there was the first version, and it could not work: that colour is
90% opaque, so a second coat took the strip's last pixels to 99% and left a *lighter rectangle*
with three straight edges. Measured on the emulator, the bar read 40, 41, 41, … 43, 43 across the
strip and **40 again from the pixel after its right edge**, with the same step above and below it —
a seam as sharp as the hard edge it replaced, which is why it survived the fade.

A ramp as wide as the distance still to scroll was the second version, and it was too wide. At
360dp it spanned 33dp, so the key *before* the cut one sat under it as well, and a 38dp rounded
rectangle at a fifth of its ink still reads as a shape. Measured, the cut key's edge stepped 72 → 47
and then straight to the bar's 40 — a soft edge on paper, a grey ghost on the screen, and a ghost is
the smudge the fade was supposed to remove. Sixteen dp of ramp lands on one key and leaves an edge.

The strip is still capped by `maxValue − value`, and the cap keeps a key that is *not* cut clean.
With the six keys fitting — 271dp of viewport against 263dp of content on the measured 411dp
screen — a constant 16dp strip lies over the last key's own edge and dims it to 65 of 72: a 7-level
step, the same artefact this chapter started from. With the cap there is nothing to draw at all,
and the key ends 72 → 40 in one pixel. The cap only bites in the last 16dp of travel.

The curve is linear, though an S-curve was the previous shape: over 16dp the difference is below
what the eye resolves, and the linear one is what the libraries use.

One build note, because it cost a compile round twice: inside the draw lambda the name `size` once
resolved to this composable's `size: KeySize` parameter rather than to the draw scope's, and reading
it answered a `Dp` — `this.size.width` is the one that is the strip's. The parameter is gone with the
measurement below, but the qualified read stays: it is the draw scope's size the strip wants.

While the panel is up the IME is put away and the terminal stops raising it on a tap
(`TerminalHolders.imeSuppressed`): two keyboards that both answer one tap would fight over one cursor.

**The keyboard** (`⌨`) raises or lowers the phone's IME on the spot. Both glyphs are Lucide borrows
(`ic_laptop.xml`, `ic_keyboard.xml`) because the Miuix set has neither a keyboard nor a
left-right arrow, and the size is the call site's decision as it is for every borrowed glyph (§12.3).

### What the two-row bar cost, and why the keys that went are gone

Two rows of ten keys each replaced a cross-shaped arrow pad on the argument that the rows were
a *reading rule* — the line being typed, then the session. The rule was right and the shape was
wrong: the reader who has to use the bar is holding a phone in one hand, and a second row puts
half the keys past the thumb's reach for a bar that exists to save taps. The keys that went
with it — `CTRL`, `ALT`, `TAB`, `PGUP`, `PGDN`, `HOME`, `END`, `C-D` and the five symbols —
are all reachable another way: `TAB` from the soft keyboard, the rest through the send-text
field or a typed command. What stayed is the six the bar draws.

### What this replaced, and why the history matters

The bar was one horizontally scrolling row of chips, arrows first as a flat `← ↑ ↓ →`, then
a two-row grid in which the arrows were a **cross** (`↑` above `↓`, `←` and `→` flanking
them) on the argument that a flat arrow row is not a d-pad: the eye reads left to right and
has to stop and count to find "down". Uniform keys did the rest of the alignment — every
cell the same rectangle, so `↑` is above `↓` because both are the middle cell, and no label
can be clipped by a neighbour's width, which four earlier attempts at measuring columns each
got wrong in a different way.

Two things ended the cross. The first is a *picture*: the bar the reader wanted has the
arrows in one row, and a request for a shape outranks a reading argument. The second is that
the row it left behind has a rule of its own — with the arrows first in a row that is explicitly
"the line being typed", counting from `↑` to `↓` is not the task, because nothing to their
right is another arrow.

**The measurement the cross was made of is gone, and it was the last piece of the two-row bar.**
Uniform keys were the cross's answer to alignment, and the uniform size was solved by measuring the
widest label with a `TextMeasurer` before the first layout; the same pass solved the horizontal
inset against the key's height, because a round cap curves into the label's box by an amount that
grows with the key — `3.06dp` at a 24dp key, `9.35dp` at a 44dp one. The one-row rewrite left that
pass computing a `KeySize` nothing read: the result reached `ExtraKeysRow` as a `size` parameter the
row never used, and the labels it measured were the *two-row* bar's sixteen — thirteen of them keys
the bar had stopped drawing, measured again on every language change. It went with the composables
that still drew them (`ScrollToggle`, `KeyButton`, `ExtraKeyChip`, `KeyGroup`, `GlyphKey`), so the bar
has no pre-layout measurement at all now: a key's width is its own label's past a 38dp floor, and its
height is one constant.

**The constant is 32dp, and it was measured rather than guessed.** On the 420dpi emulator the
label's line box is 39 px at font scale 1.0, 66 px at 1.8 and 74 px at 2.0 — 14.9dp, 25.1dp and
28.2dp — so the 32dp ink holds the largest text scale Android offers with 3.8dp to spare and never
clips its label. At 1.8 the six keys no longer fit the viewport: the row scrolls, and `ESC` waits
under the trailing fade until it is scrolled into view. That is the cost the row is designed to pay
rather than a fault to measure away, and it is why no height has to be derived from a label any
more.

**The bar was two rows tall for a long time, and the reason changed.** It was two keys tall for the
cross's sake, so a single row of keys beside the cross left half the height empty wherever that row
did not reach — and the row reached past the right edge and scrolled instead of using the space
below it. The split then was `EXTRA_KEYS_PER_ROW`, six keys under `CTRL` and `ALT`, chosen because
six is where the two rows came out within one key of each other. That was a *packing* rule and it
said nothing about what a key was for; with the cross gone there is no second row to pack, and the
constant went with the keys it packed.

**The other frame went too, and the keys are one block.** The arrow pad and the keys beside it were
two `KeyGroup`s 16dp apart, each on its own `surfaceContainerHigh` track — so what the reader saw was
one strip with a seam down it, the same raised surface at two insets, which reads as a rendering
mistake rather than as two sets of keys. One pill holds the row now, and the three action buttons
sit outside it: they are the bar's own controls rather than keys, so they are the one thing on the
bar that is *not* on the keys' track.

**The keys' height was once the label's, and one revision of it was a fix worth keeping in mind.**
The two-row chips measured their label's line box, because a flat 34dp had clipped every label at
font scale 1.8, where that revision's `labelLarge` line box was 36sp — measured on the emulator in
Japanese at 1.8: the chips drew 34dp around a 36dp box, `スクロール` was cut to `スク`, and the bar,
whose height was a constant 76dp derived from *30dp* chips two revisions behind, was 8dp shorter
than its own two rows, so the second row sat on the bar's bottom edge and was drawn over by the tab
strip that then stood below it. The height became the larger of a floor and the measured box plus
its padding, and the bar's own height was derived from two of those. The one-row bar draws one row
of 32dp keys, and the number above is why that constant is safe rather than why measuring was
wrong — the measurement answered a question the two-row bar had.

The 34dp floor that started that sequence was the composer's `ControlChip` height, adopted so the
two tappable pill families matched. At that size the keys read as oversized against the terminal's
own text: the bar is chrome around a dense monospace transcript, and a 34dp/50dp key is a composer
pill dropped into it. Two passes took them down — first the padding (11dp/7dp around the label
became 8dp/5dp, floor 28dp), then the type as well, `labelMedium` instead of `labelLarge`, because
the larger face's line box alone kept the chips near 28dp however tight the padding got. That pass
ended at 24dp with 6dp of horizontal and 4dp of vertical padding, about 36dp wide for the widest
shipped label and 8dp corners, and the bar at font scale 1.0 was about 62dp rather than the 34dp
revision's 84dp. The one-row keys are 32dp with 10dp corners and a 38dp floor: a short row can
afford a taller key, because it draws six keys rather than twenty and nothing has to fit.

**The width ceiling went with the measurement.** The bar used to cap every key's width at
`MAX_KEY_WIDTH`, and `Line break` was 72dp of `labelMedium` monospace — longer than the cap, so it
drew clipped, which is the "terminal newline button text is broken in English" report. `Newline`
was the same fact inside 51dp, and shortening the label was the fix rather than raising the cap,
because the cap was what bounded how far the row scrolled.

That answer ran out where a label had no shorter form. `スクロール` is five full-width glyphs —
measured at 184px, 70dp — so the Japanese bar against a 64dp ceiling drew `スク` and dropped the
rest of the word; the ceiling was raised to 72dp, which is where the longest label the bar ships
ends. There is no ceiling in the one-row bar: a key is as wide as its own label and the row
scrolls, so a longer translation costs a scroll step rather than a glyph. `TextOverflow.Ellipsis`
stays on the label as the marker for a constraint that should not exist — the failure it once
showed, a word cut mid-glyph with nothing to say it was longer, is what the cap's removal makes
impossible.

**The line-break key**, which the one-row bar no longer draws, sent a bare `\n` on the theory that
its job was the shell's `>` continuation prompt. That theory was wrong twice: the newline character *is* the line
delimiter in a canonical-mode terminal, so the shell read the line and ran it — "the
newline key is a carriage return" — and the missing glyph case it was written for was
never what the key was for. It was changed to send `lnext` (`0x16`, the terminal's `^V`)
followed by `\n`, so the newline was quoted and landed *inside* the line being typed: readline
handled the `^V` as `quoted-insert` in non-canonical mode, and a program reading whole lines had it
done by the line discipline. The key went with the two-row bar; a newline is typed into the
send-text sheet now, which appends nothing (§*The three round buttons*), and `\r` from the soft
keyboard still runs the line.

The scroll toggle has been through three shapes: a wide two-line button pinned to the
right of the bar, showing its state in an icon *and* a word, which made the least-used
control the most prominent thing on the bar; an icon-only square, quieter but saying
nothing; and now the word `滚动` in a key-sized cell, so it reads as one of the bar's
keys because it is one. **Only its tint changes when tapped** — `primary` while
following, `error` while paused — so it keeps its size, its word and its shape and
cannot be misread as a mode it is not.

The toggle drives the vendored `TerminalEmulator.isAutoScrollDisabled()`, whose
`onScreenUpdated` already implements what follow-off should mean — a row shift is
subtracted from `mTopRow` instead of the view snapping to the bottom — so a long
command's output accumulates *below* the part being read and nothing jumps. It is the
only mutator the emulator exposes (`toggleAutoScrollDisabled`, no setter), so the flag
is read back and flipped only when it disagrees: calling the toggle unconditionally
inverts the state on every recomposition.

The bar's choice is UI state, not emulator state, and it is mirrored into the emulator
from the `AndroidView`'s `update` block rather than only from an effect: the emulator
lives in a `View` that Compose recreates on every tab switch, and the effect has nothing
to attach to at the moment the state is first set.

### Output repaint, and why one collector is enough

A shell's output reaches the one view showing it from two directions: the session client
repaints the view it is attached to, and `ScreenRepaintAnchor` catches what arrived while
another page was on screen. The second path used to be `collectAsState()` plus a
`LaunchedEffect` keyed on the revision, so a `yes` or an unpacking archive paid — **per
64 KiB PTY read**, the session's receive buffer — a snapshot read, a recomposition of the
anchor, a cancelled-and-relaunched coroutine and an `updateSize()` before the repaint the
manager had already made. It is one `screenRevision.collect { … }` now: the latest screen
state, painted once, with no recomposition of its own and no geometry pass — the
`AndroidView`'s `update` block is where `applyViewSize()` is called.
