# 9.2. The settings pages, and the furniture of a page

*[Architecture](../ARCHITECTURE.md) §9.2.*

What the pages below the tab strip are made of: the app bar every one of them wears, the rows and cards
they are built from, and the settings pages whose content needed an argument of its own — those two
are §9.3, because this file reached the size at which a chapter is split (§9.3 is the web-access
page and the maintenance page, and nothing about them changed in the move).

## The app bar replaces the header band, and the title is one line

A settings page is a `PiScaffold` — the design system's shell, a flexible app bar over a scrolling
body (§13) — and the fixed-height band these pages used to wear is gone with it. What the band was
for survives as two rules, because they are about the *content* of the first line rather than about
the band's box:

- **The title is one line, ellipsised.** It was `titleLarge` over up to **two** lines, and that
  ceiling was itself a fix: at font scale 1.8 a 71-character title drew as `run ls /nonexist…` in the
  649 px the chat page's three action buttons leave, two lines fitted 12 characters a line at that
  scale, and a third line was 100 px of band — a quarter of the screen. A long title now ends in an
  ellipsis, and the whole of it is in the history list, whose row gives it two lines, and in the
  transcript under the bar. The collapsing bar is where a title is allowed its two lines, because
  that bar gives the height back as the reader scrolls: the chat page, the files listing and the
  manual's own document.
- **The subtitle is one line too.** It is where a multi-line pi failure used to go; the first line
  names the cause and the rest is on the Agent page.

What is left of the band's own reasoning is the *height*, and it belongs to Material now: the bar is
the pinned `MediumFlexibleTopAppBar` on every settings page, and it measures its own two lines —
136dp below the status bar on the 1080 px emulator, against the 152dp the large collapsing bar takes
before it gives any of it back (on the chat page, the files listing and the manual's own document,
the three pages whose title is the point). The band this replaced was pinned in dp instead — one
title line, one subtitle line and 8dp above and below, the whole of it 56dp at scale 1.0, derived
from the two styles' own line heights rather than written down — and it existed because the heights
*moved*: measured on the emulator before it, the chat page's band was 236 px, 63 px of status bar and
173 px of content, and it changed whenever the title or the subtitle did. A Han glyph needs more of a
line box than a Latin one at the same style (`新对话` laid out 82 px where
`run ls /nonexistent-dir-…` took 74), so the band depended on the script its title happened to start
with; the chat page's title is user data and its subtitle is the agent's state, so both changed
several times a session and everything below moved with them. A flexible bar answers that by being
allowed to move, and the page under it by not claiming a fixed number of dp.

The chat page's empty-conversation title is the application's name rather than "New conversation":
the bar says *what* the reader is looking at, and "新对话" answers a question nobody asked, since an
empty transcript is what a new conversation looks like — and it is what the hero empty state says
instead, one screen down.

## A divider is between rows, not beside a text field

The search page's key section drew a `SettingsDivider()` over each of its seven credential
fields — six between the boxes and one under the note — and thin lines appeared between the
input boxes. A divider spans its card's full width; an `OutlinedTextField` is inset 12dp
inside that card and rounds its own corners, so the line crossed the 4dp of padding above
and below the box and stuck out past both of its corners, with 4dp of air on each side of
it. Seven boxes with a full-width hairline threaded between every pair read as a layout
fault rather than as a list. The same thing happened in miniature beside the two number
fields and the two SearXNG fields, and those went with them; the fields themselves are gone
from the page now — every credential is typed into the document editor — but the rule stands
for the fields that remain.

The rule is now the design system's own row divider: `SettingsDivider` is `PiRowDivider` and
nothing else, which is what makes the inset arithmetic one number in one file. §13's
`PiRowDivider` separates *rows* — a switch, a picker, a statement of fact — and a text field
is not one, because it brings its own outline. A field
therefore follows the row above it directly, and a run of fields is separated by their
outlines and by the 8dp that two `vertical = 4.dp` paddings add up to. Nothing was lost: the
section's label and group are what say where a group starts and ends, and the model and agent
pages had been built field-after-row from the start — the search page was the outlier.

The rejected design was insetting the divider to the field's own 12dp so the two would line
up, which is what Material's list insets look like. It does not answer the complaint — the
line is still a second boundary drawn 4dp away from the field's own — and it would have been
the only inset divider in the app.

## A card's last row is the row that acts

`SettingsSection` draws its rows in a `PiGroup`, and a `Surface` clips
its content to that shape — so a row's press ripple is a rounded rectangle at the card's own
corners and a plain rectangle everywhere else, and which of the two a row gets depends on
whether anything is drawn **inside the card** below it. The search page's
*添加配置项* row showed it: its outcome note (`ConfigNote`, `已保存…`) was inside the card
under the row, so as soon as an option had been added the row's ripple changed shape — square
along the bottom edge — while the storage page's *添加文件夹* row, which never had anything
under it, kept its rounded one.

The rule that came out of it: **the notes around an action live outside its group**, above the
section (a `SettingsNote` or a `PiNotice`) or below it (the outcome), so the group is the list
and the one row that acts on it. `WebSearchConfigOptions` and `WebSearchResetSection` are both
built that way, and the two `SettingsNote`s that explain the configuration section moved out
with them. The alternative — clipping the row to a rounded rectangle of its own so its ripple
never changes — was rejected because it makes a row in the middle of a card look detached from
it: the storage page's row would still not match it in the empty state.

**That particular failure cannot recur, and the rule stays anyway.** Every row clips to
`PiShapes.row` for its own press outline, and the group's `PiShapes.card` frame is padded 4dp
above and below its first and last row, so a row's outline is a concentric rounded rectangle
whatever is drawn around it — a row at the foot of a group is no longer the row that inherits
the group's bottom corners. Measured on the search page before the change: the add row's press
outline changed after the first option was added, because the outcome note appeared inside the
card under it, while the storage page's add-a-folder row never moved. The measurement is kept
where it is worth keeping: `WebSearchConfigOptions` still records it, and the layout it argues
for is still right for the other reason — a group is rows, and a sentence is not one, and the
acting row belongs last.

## A list is a card, a row wraps before it clips, and an empty list says so

Three rules the rows and lists share, and the Files tab was the last page not following
them.

**A list is a card.** Rows sit in a `PiGroup` — a `Surface` at `PiShapes.card` — inset 12dp from the
page (`PiPagePadding`); each row is clipped to `PiShapes.row` for its own press outline, and a
`PiRowDivider` is drawn *between* two rows and never under the last one — a hairline there runs
along the group's own bottom edge. §13 is where the two radii are decided and why a row's outline is
not the frame's. A row's leading mark is a 24dp box with a 16dp gap after it, so a row's title sits
**76dp** from the screen edge — measured at density 2.625 on the 1080 px emulator, where that node
starts at x=201 — while the section label above it and any note under it sit at x=95, 36dp, which is
the column the row's mark occupies. The conversations list was the first to be fixed, and its comment
records what the flat version looked like: "no corners on the tap target, and a full-bleed hairline
that did not match the settings pages the rest of the app reads as one with". The Files tab was
still drawing exactly that — rows edge to edge, their labels 16dp from the screen instead
of the 28dp a settings row's gutter put them at then, and a rule after every row including the last.
Its listing is one such frame now, a `Surface` around the `LazyColumn` rather than a `PiGroup`
because a group holds a plain `Column` and a directory of a few thousand entries is exactly why the
rows are lazy.

**A row's text wraps before it clips.** `SettingsRow` allows its title two lines, its
subtitle three and its value two, and the subtitle's three are a measurement rather than a
preference: the backup page's category sentences are a list inside a sentence — 40
characters, `主题、语言、工作目录、工具调用守卫、共享存储授权，以及本应用保存的其他全部偏好`
— and two lines cut the end of that list off at any text size above the default. The value
column's own cap is two lines with the same reasoning at one line. A row that carries a
*machine* string — a directory path, a revision — is handed in as the subtitle rather than
as the value, because the subtitle has the row's full width and a third of a row cannot
show a path. A row's *height* is a floor as well as a consequence of its text: the sheet
rows are at least 56dp (`PICKER_ROW_MIN_HEIGHT`, which is `PiSheetRow`'s own floor), and the
folder tree the storage page draws for itself — a picker row would dismiss the sheet on every
step into a directory — is that same `PiSheetRow` and no longer a row of its own. It *was* one,
and the measurement is why it is not: it claimed to be the same row as the two rows above it
while measuring 44dp, 8dp under its twin and under the 48dp a touch target needs, so every plain
folder in the tree was the small row in its own list.

**An empty list says so.** A `listFiles()` answer that is empty and one that was refused
are the same empty list, so the Files tab draws one sentence for both; before it drew
nothing at all, which is the one answer that cannot be told from a failed load.

## A form that holds edits asks before it leaves

The model form is the one page in the app that keeps its edits until Save, which is also what
makes it the only page that can lose them. Every way out — the back arrow, the Cancel button,
the system back gesture — goes through one `requestLeave`, which leaves directly when the
form matches what is stored and otherwise asks (save / discard / cancel). The gesture needs a
handler composed *inside* the page, because the settings tab registers one for the whole tab
and the deeper handler is the one that runs first; it is `PageBackHandler` rather than
`BackHandler`, so a picker sheet still takes the gesture before the page does. The comparison
is field-by-field against the stored profile rather than a `dirty` flag set in every writer,
and it counts the model-id field's own draft: "added a model, swiped back, and the model was
not in the list" was an id typed into the field and never added, and a flag maintained by the
eight setters would have had to be right about that too.

**What the question offers depends on whether the form can be stored at all.** It used to
offer Save and Discard unconditionally, and with a profile that was not yet complete — no
provider, no model ticked — that was a loop with no exit: Save was refused, the reason was
written at the bottom of the page the dialog was still covering, and the user was back at the
same two buttons. The refusal is now `refusal()`, a pure function that both the dialog and
`save` ask, and when it answers the dialog's first button is *keep editing* and its body is
the reason; Save appears only when Save would succeed. `refusal` is pure for a reason of its
own: `save` adds the model in the id field as it stores, so a check that mutated the form
would make the *question* change the thing it is asking about.

## The update check is a row, and it is the app's only request of its own

About PiKit gained a **Check for updates** row, and the design question was not how to check
but whether to. A release check on launch would be one request per cold start, from every
install, carrying an install timestamp to GitHub — telemetry by another name, in a project
whose whole claim is that it has none. So the row is inert until it is tapped, the answer is
shown in the row it was asked from, and there is no update *notification*, because a
notification is a check nobody asked for. Four details are each a decision:

- **It asks GitHub Releases, not a version file of the app's own.** The release workflow
  already creates the release and the tag, so the answer is the artefact a user would
  install. This is the distribution channel: Play is not one — the app targets `targetSdk
  28` (see §1) and holds `MANAGE_EXTERNAL_STORAGE`, which Play's policies do not accept for
  this kind of app. `pikit.repository` is the one line that says which repository to ask.
- **It compares `versionName`, not `versionCode`.** Android's integer ordering is a fact
  about installability, not about which release is newer to a person, and the tag is derived
  from `versionName` anyway: `0.10.0` is newer than `0.9.0`, which a string comparison gets
  backwards.
- **It asks the release *page*, not the API.** `GET api.github.com/repos/…/releases/latest`
  is unauthenticated here — there is no token in this app, by design — and GitHub allows
  **60 such requests per hour per address**, so an address behind a VPN can already have spent
  a whole node's budget: measured from exactly such an address, `HTTP/1.1 403 rate limit exceeded`
  with `X-RateLimit-Limit: 60`, `X-RateLimit-Remaining: 0` and `X-RateLimit-Used: 60`. The page
  `https://github.com/<owner>/<repo>/releases/latest` carries the same fact as a `302` to
  `…/releases/tag/v0.2.0`, against no API budget, and the URL it lands on is both the tag to
  compare and the page to open; a repository with nothing published answers `404` (measured
  while this project had no release) or redirects to the releases list (measured on
  `octocat/Hello-World`).
- **It installs nothing.** Downloading and relaunching an APK from inside the app needs
  `REQUEST_INSTALL_PACKAGES`, a `FileProvider` root and a `PackageInstaller` session, and it
  only works when the new build is signed with the same key as the installed one. The row
  opens the release page in a browser instead; the user installs it, which is one tap more
  and three failure modes fewer.

Its failure text is the exception's message — or a named refusal — rather than a guess:
"Unable to resolve host" is a different problem from "GitHub refused the request (403)", and
the person reading it is the one who can act on the difference. `data/UpdateCheck.kt` is the
whole of it: no library, one `HttpURLConnection` that follows the redirect, and two pure
functions (`releaseFrom`, `compareVersions`) that the JVM suite pins.

**The row is one status line, and the page carries no standing paragraph under it.** A
`SettingsNote` used to sit under the application section and state the three facts above in
three sentences — asked only when tapped, opens the release page, downloads nothing — on the
grounds that a page which names the traffic it makes is what lets the rest of the app claim
it makes none. That note was the second thing to read after the row it described, and it
repeated decisions this chapter already records; the row's own idle line is the one-line
answer ("到 GitHub 查看新版本"), and a check that *fails* is the only case that grows a
note under the section, carrying the exception's message. The idle line is written to a
one-line budget for the same reason the failure reason is no longer in the subtitle: the
row must not resize when its status is read.

## The icon choice is a component switch, because that is what a launcher icon is

The personalization page offers two marks — white on black, and black on
white — as two `PiChoiceRow`s in a group, and it is the only choice in the app whose effect the app
does not draw. Android reads
a launcher icon out of an app's **manifest** and has no API that changes one at run time, so a
choice between two icons is a choice between two *entry points*: the manifest declares one
`activity-alias` per icon, both targeting `MainActivity`, and exactly one of them is enabled.
`LauncherIcon` names the two aliases and `applyLauncherIcon` is the switch, and that is why the
activity itself carries no `MAIN`/`LAUNCHER` filter any more — with one it would be a third
home-screen entry beside the aliases.

Both marks are visible at once rather than behind a picker sheet, and that is the same argument as
the three themes above them: a set of *names* is better read at once than one at a time behind a
scrim, and the row that was just tapped is the row that changes when the choice lands — the live
preview the sheet hid behind its own scrim. One thing the sheet could do that a group of choice rows
cannot, and it is worth naming: a picker can carry a paragraph under its list, so the icon footnote is
a `PiNote` under the group rather than a line inside the choice.

The packaged APK is the check rather than the source, and it is cheap: `aapt2 dump xmltree
--file AndroidManifest.xml` on a debug APK reports `pi.kit.mob.ui.LauncherDark` with
`android:enabled=true` and `@mipmap/ic_launcher`, `pi.kit.mob.ui.LauncherLight` with
`android:enabled=false` and `@mipmap/ic_launcher_light`, and the `activity` element with no
intent filter at all.

Four details are each a decision:

- **Enabled before disabled.** The two calls happen in that order because the other one leaves a
  moment in which neither alias is on, and a launcher that notices has just been told the app
  has no entry point.
- **`DONT_KILL_APP`.** Switching cosmetics is not a reason to tear down a running agent, so the
  process is not killed for it. The cost is real and is stated in the note under the group rather
  than left as a surprise: the task the change was made from belongs to an alias that is now off, so
  the launcher is free to re-create the icon somewhere else on the home screen.
- **Only the entry moves.** `<application android:icon>` is untouched, so the recent-tasks card,
  the app's own page in system settings and the uninstall dialog keep the black mark whichever
  icon is chosen. Those surfaces have one icon and no per-entry notion, and the alternative is
  the app rewriting its identity rather than offering a preference.
- **A fresh install writes nothing.** `getComponentEnabledSetting` answers
  `COMPONENT_ENABLED_STATE_DEFAULT` for a component nobody has touched, and that means "whatever
  the manifest declared" rather than "enabled" — so the enum carries each alias's declared state,
  and the first launch of a new install makes no call at all. That same call is what re-states
  the choice on every later launch, where it is a no-op unless the installed aliases have
  drifted from the preference.

The two icons are one mark, and nothing in the platform can make one vector take another's
geometry: `ic_launcher_foreground_light.xml` is `ic_launcher_foreground.xml`'s path data
character for character, and the two files differ in `android:fillColor` alone. A second copy is
a thing that drifts, so `tools/render-icon.py` compares them on every run — in both modes, not
only under `--check` — and refuses to write anything when the path data, the `fillType` or the
group's translation disagree, or when the white icon's ink *is* the field it is drawn on. That
last case is the one the comparison has to catch rather than describe: a white mark on a white
field is a blank square, and nothing about either file alone says so.

## The text size is one multiplier, and its slider commits on release

The personalization page's **Text size** row is seven steps, the fourth
(`1.0`) the default, and the row reports the step as a percentage in a `PiBadge` beside the chevron
that opens the sheet the `Slider` lives in. The slider's two ends are the same `A` glyph at 11sp and
at 20sp. The ends are a *sample* rather than the words "small" and "large": they need no
translation, and because they are `sp` they grow with the choice.

It was a slider on the row itself, then a slider that expanded on it; the sheet is the third shape
and the one that stays, because it makes this control the same *kind* of thing as the two choices
above it while keeping the one thing a slider is for — a range, whose alternatives are worth seeing
side by side under the finger. Seven tappable numbers in a group instead would throw that away: a
size is the one setting here that is not a set of names. The note under the track is the catalog's
own subtitle, because the sheet is where "松手即生效" is read.

Three decisions, and the second one is a measurement:

- **One multiplier on `LocalDensity`, not nine type styles.** `MainActivity` provides
  `Density(density, fontScale * step)` above the theme, so every `sp` in the app moves together —
  the transcript, the terminal banner, the settings rows and the dialogs (a Compose `Dialog` runs
  in the parent composition and inherits the local). It multiplies the *platform's* `fontScale`
  rather than replacing it, so a device already set to 1.3 keeps that and gains the step on top;
  `density` itself is passed through, because the row above measured its paddings in it. The range
  is 0.80–1.30: the layout measurements in this chapter were taken at font scale 1.8 and that is
  where a row's label starts being clipped, not where it looks best, and 0.80 leaves 12sp captions
  at 9.6sp, which is the floor of what stays legible.
- **The drag moves a draft and commits on release.** Applying each step as the finger passed it
  re-measures the entire interface mid-gesture, and that is what breaks: measured on the
  emulator, a drag across the whole track with nine `input motionevent MOVE`s advanced **one**
  step (80% → 90%), while a single MOVE of the same total distance moved four (90% → 130%). The
  mapping was never the problem — being re-measured under the pointer was. So the slider drives a
  local draft (the thumb, the ticks and the percentage, all of which are the sheet's own) and the
  step is committed in `onValueChangeFinished`, which is one re-measure per gesture. It is also
  why the subtitle says 松手即生效 rather than "takes effect immediately": the number follows the
  finger and the text follows the release, and the row's own badge is written from the committed
  step rather than from the draft.
- **A sheet, where every other choice on this page is a row of alternatives.** The theme and the
  launcher mark are both visible at once — a group of three and a group of two, the current one
  filled with `secondaryContainer` and an emphasized title — and a *range* is the one kind of choice
  that cannot be shown that way: a picker hides its alternatives until it is opened, which is right
  for a list of *names* (a language, a model) and wrong for a size, and seven rows of one number each
  is what the range would look like as a list. The slider therefore keeps the sheet, and the row
  keeps the badge that reports the step and the chevron that opens it — which is also the one row on
  the page whose trailing slot is a value *and* a way in.

## The search row carries the extension's version

The tab root's `搜索设置` row is the row that names the feature, so it also carries the one fact
about it that would otherwise be a terminal away: the version of the bundled `pi-web-access`
extension, read from the image's own metadata (`BundledExtension.version`, the same string the
search page draws on its master switch) and shown monospaced in the value column, exactly as the
maintenance row shows pi's version above it. On a measured install that is `0.35.0` beside
`1.0.1` for pi and `0.3.1` for the app: three versions, one per thing that has one, each on the
row that owns it. An image without the extension leaves the value empty rather than printing a
placeholder — the search page itself says `searchNotBundled` instead of inventing a version.
